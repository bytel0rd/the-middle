# Why Nigeria Shouldn't Copy the East Asian Development Model

A recent social media discussion caught my attention.

Someone remarked, almost casually, that "Japanese trains are always on time." The point being made was that societies where things consistently work—where infrastructure functions, public services are reliable, and social organization appears seamless—represent a form of cultural superiority.

I understand the appeal of that argument.

There is something impressive about societies where trains run on schedule, streets are clean, institutions are dependable, and social norms produce predictable outcomes. It is easy to look at those results and conclude that they represent a superior way of organizing society.

But I don't think Nigeria should aim to become that kind of society, at least not by copying the East Asian model.

## Nigeria's Advantage Is Different

The strength of many East Asian societies lies in coordination, conformity, and adherence to established systems. Individuals are expected to operate within highly structured frameworks.

Nigeria's comparative advantage is almost the opposite.

We are a society where people are expected to take initiative for themselves. We are adaptive, improvisational, and remarkably capable of finding solutions in environments where formal systems are weak.

This difference is often mistaken for incompetence when, in reality, it reflects a different set of incentives and social expectations.

## The Problem Is Not Capacity

Making trains run on time is not inherently difficult.

If necessary, Nigerians would find a way to make it happen. A Nigerian will hold two cables together to keep a system running if that is what the situation requires. The issue is rarely a lack of capacity.

The real issue is incentives.

When systems fail in Nigeria, they usually do not fail because people are incapable of making them work. They fail because the social, political, and economic incentives surrounding those systems do not consistently reward reliability.

This distinction matters.

If Nigerians can successfully operate complex organizations such as banks, large religious institutions, and multinational businesses within the country's challenging environment, then the problem is not human capability. Nigerians clearly possess the talent and organizational capacity required.

The challenge is creating systems where reliable performance is consistently incentivized.

## Why Centralized Systems Struggle

Another observation about Nigeria is that centralized systems tend to perform worse than decentralized ones.

The country's survival has depended on millions of individuals, families, communities, and businesses creating their own solutions to systemic failures. People generate their own electricity, organize their own security arrangements, create informal logistics networks, and build alternative support systems when public institutions fall short.

In many ways, Nigeria functions because people patch the gaps themselves.

This is why importing highly centralized models of social organization may not fit our realities. The kind of social conformity that allows certain societies to operate with remarkable efficiency is not naturally embedded in Nigeria's social structure.

That does not mean we should embrace dysfunction. It means we should build institutions that align with how Nigerians actually organize themselves.

## Development Is Gradual

There is also a tendency to treat development as if it can be imported wholesale.

It cannot.

Strong institutions are the product of gradual evolution. Before a society reaches the stage where systems operate with near-perfect reliability, it must first build the foundations that support those outcomes.

The focus should be on strengthening weak institutions, improving accountability, and creating better incentives. Institutional maturity follows from that process; it cannot be skipped.

## The Diversity Challenge

One factor often overlooked in development discussions is social diversity.

Many countries that achieved rapid economic transformation were relatively homogeneous societies. Shared language, culture, history, and social expectations lowered the cost of coordination and made collective action easier.

Nigeria is different.

Our society is extraordinarily diverse, not only in ethnicity but also in culture, religion, geography, and local identity. Development in such an environment becomes a process of constant negotiation.

Every additional stakeholder increases the complexity of reaching consensus on systems, priorities, and resource allocation.

In this sense, development is fundamentally a coordination problem.

The more participants involved, the greater the risk of failure.

## Why Decentralization Matters

For a country as diverse as Nigeria, decentralization is not merely an administrative preference. It may be a developmental necessity.

The path to trains running on time is not blind conformity or rigid central planning. It is the creation of layers of operational excellence distributed across institutions, regions, and communities.

Nigeria's challenge is not becoming more like Japan.

Nigeria's challenge is figuring out how a highly diverse society can develop at scale while preserving the flexibility and adaptability that have allowed it to survive.

There is no established blueprint for this.

Not Japan. Not South Korea. Not Singapore. Not even India provides a complete template.

Nigeria's development model will likely have to be uniquely Nigerian.

And that means understanding that our greatest strength may not be conformity, but adaptation.

## Every Successful Society Makes Trade-Offs

Another thing that becomes obvious the more you study developed societies is that there is no free lunch.

Every successful society makes trade-offs.

Singapore's success did not emerge in a vacuum. Citizens accepted a stronger state and greater restrictions in certain areas of public and private life in exchange for security, order, and economic prosperity.

Japan's social cohesion also comes with trade-offs. The efficiency and predictability that outsiders admire are partly supported by strong social expectations and pressure toward conformity. Individual initiative is often balanced against maintaining group harmony and social order.

Whether one agrees with these arrangements or not, the important point is that every model of development extracts a price.

The mistake many people make is looking only at the outcomes while ignoring the trade-offs that produced them.

As a Nigerian, I am not convinced that either model can simply be transplanted here.

We have already experienced what happens when individual and property rights are weak. Few Nigerians would argue that this has produced an especially prosperous or well-governed society.

Likewise, the idea of reducing individual initiative in exchange for greater social conformity raises another question: conformity to what, and under whose authority?

In societies with strong institutions, conformity may reinforce systems that broadly work. In societies where institutions are still developing, excessive conformity can simply concentrate power in the hands of ineffective or unaccountable actors.

The risk is obvious. Imagine asking Nigerians to surrender the culture of individual initiative that has allowed millions of people to survive institutional failures, while placing even greater authority in the hands of governments and institutions that have not yet earned that trust.

That is not a development strategy. It is a recipe for stagnation.

Nigeria's challenge is therefore not choosing between freedom and development, or between initiative and order. It is figuring out how to build effective institutions while preserving the entrepreneurial, adaptive, and self-starting culture that already exists within society.

The goal should not be to become a copy of Japan, Singapore, or any other successful country.

The goal should be to understand the trade-offs they made, decide which ones fit our realities, and build a model that reflects Nigeria's own strengths and constraints.

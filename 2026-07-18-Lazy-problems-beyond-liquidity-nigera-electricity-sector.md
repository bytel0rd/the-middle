# Lazy problems : Beyond Liquidity - Rethinking Nigeria's Electricity Sector Through Productivity and Industrial Capability

## Introduction

For decades, Nigeria's electricity debate has been dominated by one recurring theme: **liquidity**.

Industry experts, regulators and policymakers argue that the sector suffers from a liquidity crisis because electricity tariffs are not sufficiently cost-reflective. As a result, Generation Companies (GenCos) cannot purchase enough gas, Transmission Company of Nigeria (TCN) cannot adequately maintain and expand the grid, and Distribution Companies (DisCos) struggle with revenue collection and infrastructure investment.

Consequently, the proposed solutions are almost always the same:

- increase tariffs;
- improve collections;
- settle debts across the value chain;
- attract more private investment.

While these are important operational issues, they reflect a much deeper misunderstanding.

The Nigerian electricity problem is **not primarily a liquidity problem.**

It is a **sustainability problem.**

More importantly, Nigeria has adopted an incomplete definition of sustainability.

Today, sustainability is almost entirely defined in financial terms:

> Can electricity companies remain profitable?

This essay argues that this is the wrong objective.

A truly sustainable electricity sector is one that continuously increases the productive capacity of the entire economy while progressively reducing the country's dependence on foreign technology, foreign expertise and foreign exchange.

In other words,

> **Liquidity is not sustainability.**

Likewise,

> **Infrastructure should not be judged by whether it pays for itself, but by whether it increases national productivity by more than it costs.**

---

# The False Assumption Behind Nigeria's Electricity Debate

Virtually every discussion surrounding Nigeria's electricity sector follows the same logic.

Low tariffs lead to poor collections.

Poor collections produce liquidity shortages.

Liquidity shortages prevent investment.

Poor investment produces inadequate electricity supply.

Therefore:

> Increase tariffs until every participant becomes profitable.

The entire conversation assumes one fundamental principle:

> **Profitability equals sustainability.**

This assumption deserves to be challenged.

---

# Infrastructure Exists to Enable Economies, Not Maximize Profit

Electricity is fundamentally different from ordinary commercial products.

Its purpose is not to generate profit.

Its purpose is to enable production.

Electricity powers:

- factories;
- hospitals;
- schools;
- transportation;
- digital infrastructure;
- agriculture;
- research;
- households.

Without electricity, nearly every productive sector becomes less efficient.

Electricity therefore belongs in the same category as:

- highways;
- railways;
- ports;
- airports;
- water systems;
- broadband infrastructure.

These are enabling infrastructures.

Their economic value lies primarily in what they make possible.

---

# Infrastructure Can Be Financially Loss-Making Yet Economically Profitable

One of the biggest misconceptions in infrastructure policy is the belief that every infrastructure project must recover its own costs.

This is rarely true.

Suppose Nigeria spends ₦500 billion every year subsidizing electricity.

The electricity utilities themselves may never become profitable.

However, imagine that affordable electricity enables:

- ₦6 trillion in additional manufacturing output;
- millions of new jobs;
- higher exports;
- lower inflation;
- increased tax revenues;
- greater foreign investment;
- higher labour productivity.

The electricity sector loses ₦500 billion.

The Nigerian economy gains several trillion naira.

The investment is therefore economically profitable despite being financially loss-making.

This is how governments evaluate many forms of infrastructure.

Nobody asks whether a highway should generate profits equal to its construction cost.

Nobody expects a national road network to maximize shareholder returns.

The purpose of the road is commerce.

Electricity should be viewed through the same lens.

---

# International Experience Supports This View

Many countries have historically invested heavily in infrastructure without expecting immediate commercial profitability.

China's extensive railway, electricity and transport investments were justified primarily because they accelerated:

- industrialization;
- exports;
- urbanization;
- manufacturing;
- productivity.

Similarly, many high-speed rail systems around the world recover their operating expenses but never fully recover their original construction costs.

Their justification lies in wider economic benefits rather than direct financial returns.

Infrastructure is therefore often an investment in national productivity rather than a profit-maximizing enterprise.

---

# Nigeria's Real Problem Is Not Liquidity

Liquidity matters.

Infrastructure cannot function indefinitely without money.

However, Nigeria's problem extends far beyond cash flow.

The country has confused **financial sustainability** with **national sustainability.**

These are not the same thing.

---

# Liquidity Is Only One Dimension of Sustainability

Financial liquidity answers one question:

> Can today's bills be paid?

Sustainability asks a much broader question:

> Can this system continue functioning efficiently for decades while becoming progressively stronger?

These are fundamentally different questions.

---

# Financial Sustainability

Financial sustainability concerns whether:

- generators can purchase gas;
- transmission companies can fund maintenance;
- distribution companies can cover operating costs.

This is the dimension that dominates Nigeria's policy discussions.

It is important.

But it is incomplete.

---

# Technical Sustainability

A sustainable electricity sector must also possess sufficient technical capability.

Questions include:

- Can engineers maintain the grid?
- Can failures be repaired locally?
- Can equipment be upgraded without excessive external dependence?

Without technical capability, financial liquidity merely delays future problems.

---

# Industrial Sustainability

The third dimension is almost entirely absent from Nigeria's electricity debate.

Industrial sustainability asks:

> Does every electricity project leave Nigeria more capable than before?

Every major infrastructure investment should increase domestic capability.

Each project should create:

- more engineers;
- stronger local firms;
- better manufacturing capacity;
- improved technical standards;
- greater engineering knowledge.

If each project simply imports foreign equipment and foreign expertise, the country owns more assets but gains very little capability.

---

# Foreign Exchange Sustainability

The final dimension concerns foreign exchange dependence.

Much of Nigeria's electricity infrastructure depends upon imported:

- transformers;
- switchgear;
- protection systems;
- SCADA equipment;
- spare parts;
- engineering services;
- software.

Consequently, every major depreciation of the Naira permanently increases maintenance costs.

This means that even if today's liquidity crisis were completely solved, tomorrow's maintenance costs would continue rising as foreign exchange pressures intensify.

The system may be liquid.

It is still not sustainable.

---

# Liquidity Can Exist Without Sustainability

Imagine that tomorrow Nigeria receives unlimited funding.

Every GenCo is paid.

Every gas supplier is settled.

Every transmission project is completed.

Every DisCo receives sufficient investment.

Has Nigeria solved its electricity problem?

Not necessarily.

Twenty years later, Nigeria may still depend upon imported:

- transformers;
- turbines;
- meters;
- switchgear;
- protection relays;
- software;
- consultants;
- spare parts.

Every expansion.

Every repair.

Every maintenance cycle.

Every replacement.

Still requires foreign exchange.

The liquidity solved today's problems.

It did not solve tomorrow's dependence.

Liquidity is therefore **necessary**, but it is **not sufficient** for sustainability.

---

# The Missing Ingredient: Domestic Capability

The most overlooked aspect of Nigeria's electricity strategy is domestic capability.

Infrastructure should not merely produce electricity.

It should produce knowledge.

Every project should leave behind:

- better engineers;
- stronger manufacturers;
- improved supply chains;
- more capable institutions.

Infrastructure should accumulate capability.

---

# Infrastructure Should Be a National Engineering School

One of the defining characteristics of successful industrial economies is that they use infrastructure projects as opportunities to develop domestic capability.

Infrastructure is not merely consumption.

It is education.

It is industrial policy.

It is capability accumulation.

Every project should answer questions such as:

- What technologies did Nigerians learn?
- Which components can now be manufactured locally?
- Which firms became globally competitive?
- Which engineering problems were solved domestically?

These questions are rarely asked in Nigeria.

---

# China's Experience Illustrates Capability Accumulation

China did not begin by manufacturing world-class tunnel boring machines.

When China first embarked on massive underground railway construction, it relied heavily on imported machines from Germany and other advanced economies.

However, China did not treat these purchases as permanent dependence.

Instead, infrastructure projects became vehicles for:

- technology transfer;
- engineering education;
- domestic manufacturing;
- industrial learning.

Within two decades, Chinese companies became among the world's leading manufacturers of tunnel boring machines.

The lesson is not simply that China now manufactures these machines.

The lesson is that **every infrastructure project became an opportunity to reduce future dependence.**

The same philosophy guided China's development of:

- high-speed rail;
- power equipment;
- telecommunications;
- shipbuilding.

Infrastructure became industrial capability.

---

# Buying Infrastructure Is Different from Learning to Build Infrastructure

Nigeria has generally approached infrastructure as procurement.

Projects are awarded.

Equipment is imported.

Foreign contractors install systems.

Foreign firms provide maintenance.

Foreign suppliers provide replacement parts.

The country acquires infrastructure.

It does not necessarily acquire capability.

There is an important distinction between:

> Buying technology

and

> Learning to build technology.

Only the latter creates long-term sustainability.

---

# Why Manufacturing Matters

The objective is not simply to reduce imports.

Manufacturing creates a learning process.

When Nigerian firms build components:

- they discover weaknesses;
- redesign products;
- improve quality;
- train engineers;
- build supply chains;
- accumulate experience.

Capability improves through repetition.

Countries rarely become technologically advanced before they begin manufacturing.

They become technologically advanced because they manufacture continuously and improve continuously.

You cannot improve what you never build.

---

# Industrialization Is a Progressive Process

No country begins by manufacturing every component.

Nigeria should progressively increase domestic participation.

Initially, this may include:

- transmission towers;
- conductors;
- distribution transformers;
- smart meters;
- electrical panels;
- concrete poles.

Over time, capability expands into increasingly sophisticated equipment.

Industrial capability is cumulative.

Each generation builds upon the previous one.

---

# A Different Definition of Success

Nigeria should therefore evaluate electricity policy differently.

Instead of asking:

> Are electricity companies profitable?

The country should ask:

> Has electricity made Nigeria substantially more productive than it cost?

Likewise, every project should answer:

- How much domestic capability was created?
- How much future foreign exchange dependence was eliminated?
- Which industries became more competitive?
- Which technologies are now locally understood?
- Which firms are now capable of building what previously had to be imported?

These questions measure sustainability far better than profitability alone.

---

# Conclusion

Nigeria's electricity challenge is frequently described as a liquidity crisis.

Liquidity is undeniably important.

However, liquidity is only one component of sustainability.

A financially liquid electricity sector that remains permanently dependent on imported technology, imported expertise and imported maintenance is not truly sustainable.

Likewise, infrastructure should not be evaluated solely by whether utilities generate accounting profits.

Its real purpose is to increase national productivity.

Electricity enables manufacturing.

It enables transportation.

It enables education.

It enables healthcare.

It enables technological innovation.

Its economic value therefore extends far beyond the balance sheets of electricity companies.

The objective of electricity policy should not be to maximize the profitability of the electricity sector itself.

It should be to maximize the productivity of the Nigerian economy while ensuring that every investment leaves the country more technologically capable than before.

A nation becomes truly energy secure not when its electricity companies are profitable, but when its electricity infrastructure continuously strengthens its economy **and** progressively reduces its dependence on foreign technology, foreign expertise and foreign exchange.

That—not liquidity alone—is sustainability.

# Why Nigeria Is Fighting the Wrong War

Most Nigerians think of national security in military terms.

We think about soldiers, terrorists, bandits, kidnappers, intelligence agencies, and police operations. We discuss weapons procurement, military deployments, and security budgets. When insecurity rises, the instinctive response is to ask for more troops, more equipment, and more force.

But what if we have misunderstood the problem?

What if the greatest threat to Nigeria's security is not the armed man in the forest, but the financial decisions made in air-conditioned offices hundreds of kilometres away?

What if we are fighting the symptoms while neglecting the disease?

## The Crime Behind the Crimes

When people think of crime, they often focus on the final act.

The kidnapping.

The terrorist attack.

The cyber fraud.

The armed robbery.

The bandit raid.

Yet every crime has a precursor.

Every criminal organisation requires funding.

Every terrorist group requires financing.

Every kidnapping operation requires logistics.

Every organised criminal enterprise requires money.

Money is the common denominator.

This is why financial crime should not be viewed as a separate category of crime. It is often the enabling infrastructure upon which many other forms of criminal activity depend.

The question is therefore not simply:

"Who committed the crime?"

The more important question is:

"What conditions made the crime possible?"

## Understanding the Production of Crime

Most law-enforcement institutions focus on crimes after they occur.

A criminal commits an offence.

The state investigates.

The criminal is arrested.

The courts prosecute.

This approach is necessary, but it addresses only the final stage of a much larger process.

A more important question is:

Why are criminals being produced in the first place?

Consider a young graduate without opportunities.

Consider communities without functioning schools.

Consider families struggling with rising living costs.

Consider regions where economic opportunities have collapsed.

None of these conditions automatically produce criminals.

However, they create environments in which criminal markets become more attractive.

Cybercrime does not emerge from nowhere.

Kidnapping does not emerge from nowhere.

Banditry does not emerge from nowhere.

Terrorist recruitment does not emerge from nowhere.

These phenomena emerge from social, economic, and institutional conditions that make criminal activity viable.

If we wish to reduce crime, we must understand how crime is manufactured.

## The Most Expensive Crime in Nigeria

Public discussions about corruption often focus on money.

Money stolen.

Money diverted.

Money laundered.

But corruption is not merely the theft of money.

Corruption is the destruction of future economic activity.

When funds allocated for schools disappear, fewer skilled workers enter the economy.

When healthcare funding disappears, productivity declines.

When infrastructure projects are abandoned, businesses face higher costs and create fewer jobs.

When housing schemes intended for ordinary citizens are captured by politically connected individuals, wealth creation becomes concentrated rather than distributed.

The damage extends far beyond the value of the stolen funds.

The larger cost is the prosperity that never materialises.

## The Collapse of Developmental Institutions

A country does not become insecure overnight.

The process is gradual.

Schools become underfunded.

Hospitals deteriorate.

Infrastructure projects stall.

Housing becomes unaffordable.

Public institutions weaken.

Opportunities disappear.

The middle class shrinks.

Economic desperation expands.

The result is not merely poverty.

The result is a growing population of citizens who are excluded from meaningful economic participation.

This is where the relationship between corruption and insecurity becomes visible.

The destruction of developmental institutions creates the conditions from which insecurity grows.

## The Economics of Insecurity

A country does not become insecure overnight.

The process is gradual.

Schools become underfunded.

Hospitals deteriorate.

Infrastructure projects stall.

Housing becomes unaffordable.

Public institutions weaken.

Opportunities disappear.

Economic frustration grows.

The result is not merely poverty.

The result is the creation of conditions in which criminal markets become increasingly attractive.

A young person who cannot access quality education faces fewer opportunities.

A graduate who cannot find employment faces mounting economic pressure.

A community without functioning infrastructure struggles to attract investment.

A region without meaningful economic activity becomes vulnerable to criminal enterprises that can provide income, however destructive.

This does not mean poverty automatically produces crime.

Most poor people do not become criminals.

However, it would be equally naïve to ignore the relationship between economic deprivation and criminal recruitment.

Criminal organisations compete in labour markets just like legitimate businesses.

They recruit.

They train.

They compensate.

They offer incentives.

The difference is that their products are violence, fraud, extortion, and instability.

The more opportunities disappear from the legitimate economy, the easier it becomes for criminal enterprises to recruit from the illegitimate one.

This is why corruption and insecurity should never be discussed separately.

The money stolen from development today often reappears tomorrow as a security problem.


## Why Financial Crime Is a National Security Issue

Many of Nigeria's most serious security challenges possess strong financial foundations.

Kidnapping is financially motivated.

Banditry is financially motivated.

Human trafficking is financially motivated.

Illegal resource extraction is financially motivated.

Cybercrime is financially motivated.

Even terrorism, despite its ideological dimensions, depends heavily on financial networks for recruitment, logistics, procurement, and operational sustainability.

Money connects these activities.

Remove the money and many criminal enterprises become significantly weaker.

This is why institutions responsible for investigating corruption, money laundering, illicit financial flows, and financial intelligence should be viewed as strategic national security institutions.

The battlefield is not only physical.

It is financial.

## Why the EFCC May Be More Important Than We Think

This is where the discussion becomes uncomfortable.

Nigeria often treats financial crime as a secondary issue compared to terrorism, insurgency, or violent crime.

Yet the institution responsible for disrupting illicit financial activity may be one of the most strategically important institutions in the country.

Not because it arrests cybercriminals.

Not because it conducts high-profile raids.

But because it sits closest to the financial arteries that sustain both corruption and organised criminal activity.

A commission capable of effectively tracing illicit financial flows, investigating public-sector embezzlement, monitoring procurement fraud, and disrupting money laundering networks is not merely fighting corruption.

It is protecting national security.

The challenge is that public attention is frequently drawn toward visible crimes rather than systemic ones.

The cybercriminal is visible.

The kidnapper is visible.

The terrorist is visible.

The procurement fraud that deprived thousands of citizens of education, housing, healthcare, or employment opportunities is often invisible.

Yet the latter may create more long-term damage than the former.

## Fighting Causes Instead of Symptoms

A society that focuses exclusively on arresting criminals will always remain busy.

A society that focuses on preventing the conditions that produce criminals has a chance to break the cycle.

The objective should not merely be prosecution.

The objective should be prevention.

Transparent procurement systems.

Digital audit trails.

Real-time expenditure monitoring.

Institutional accountability.

The most successful anti-corruption systems are not those that produce the most arrests.

They are those that make corruption increasingly difficult to commit.

## Conclusion

Nigeria's security challenges are often discussed as though they exist independently of economic governance.

They do not.

The corruption that weakens schools today can become the insecurity of tomorrow.

The embezzlement that destroys economic opportunity today can become criminal recruitment tomorrow.

The financial crime ignored today can become a national security crisis tomorrow.

If we continue treating insecurity as purely a military problem, we will continue fighting the symptoms.

The real battle begins much earlier.

It begins wherever public resources intended for national development are diverted, captured, or stolen.

That is where many of Nigeria's security problems are born.

And that is where they must ultimately be defeated.

# Beyond Racism: The Class Dynamics Behind Anti-Nigerian Sentiment

A significant number of Nigerians believe they are profiled, disliked, or discriminated against abroad because of racism and xenophobia. While racism undoubtedly exists, I believe the reality is more complex and, in many ways, more troubling.

At its core, much of what Nigerians experience internationally is not simply racial discrimination. It is often a reflection of socio-economic hierarchy. Race may shape the form of discrimination, but class and economic power frequently determine who receives respect and who does not.

## The Protective Bubble of the Elite

Nigerian political and economic elites rarely experience the realities faced by ordinary Nigerians abroad.

They travel differently. They stay in premium hotels, live in affluent neighbourhoods, interact with influential networks, and move through spaces designed for people of wealth and status. Even in societies where racism or class prejudice exists, these attitudes are often concealed when directed toward individuals with significant economic power.

As a result, many elites operate within a protected bubble. Their experience of the world is fundamentally different from that of the average Nigerian.

This distinction matters because ordinary Nigerians often encounter a completely different social reality.

## The Hidden Structure Behind Racism

In many societies, racism operates within a broader class structure.

There is typically an elite class, a middle class, and a lower economic class. The elite often maintain a degree of cultural distance from everyone below them. The middle class, in turn, frequently directs frustration and resentment toward those with less social standing, including immigrants.

This creates a social hierarchy in which power flows downward and contempt follows the same direction.

Immigrants frequently occupy vulnerable positions within this hierarchy. They become convenient targets for economic anxiety, political frustration, and social insecurity.

This does not eliminate racism as a factor. Rather, it suggests that racism often operates alongside class interests rather than independently from them.

## Why Immigrants Become Political Targets

When governments fail to deliver economic growth, employment opportunities, affordable housing, or public security, political leaders often search for easier explanations.

Immigrants become convenient scapegoats.

Instead of addressing structural economic problems, public discourse shifts toward blaming outsiders for declining opportunities and social tensions. This strategy is politically effective because it transforms legitimate economic frustrations into cultural conflicts.

Whenever immigration becomes a dominant political talking point, it is often worth asking whether leaders are avoiding more difficult conversations about economic performance.

Scarcity encourages competition. Prosperity reduces it.

The real challenge for governments should be expanding opportunities, not encouraging citizens to fight over limited resources.

## Nigeria's Internal Contradiction

Ironically, Nigeria suffers from many of the same class dynamics it often criticizes elsewhere.

The difference is that these structures are not yet fully institutionalized.

Nigeria remains one of the few societies where meaningful socio-economic mobility is still possible for many citizens. A person born into modest circumstances can still achieve significant economic advancement through education, entrepreneurship, or professional success.

However, this potential is increasingly threatened by corruption and institutional failure.

The greatest threat to Nigerian prosperity is not external discrimination. It is the systematic diversion of resources that should be creating opportunities for citizens.

## Corruption as the Root Cause

Public discussions about crime often focus on visible symptoms: cybercrime, kidnapping, armed robbery, terrorism, and insecurity.

Yet many of these problems share a common origin.

When public funds intended for education, healthcare, housing, infrastructure, and job creation are diverted, the social consequences eventually emerge elsewhere. Economic frustration becomes insecurity. Lack of opportunity becomes criminality. Institutional weakness becomes national instability.

The real battle against crime begins long before crimes are committed.

It begins with protecting public resources and ensuring that institutions function as intended.

A serious anti-corruption strategy should focus not only on punishment but also on prevention through transparency, accountability, and digital audit systems that make large-scale abuse more difficult.

## Why Economic Strength Matters

Countries with strong economies possess more than wealth. They possess leverage.

Citizens of prosperous societies have greater freedom to choose where they work, live, and travel. They are less vulnerable to exploitation because they are not forced into unfavorable circumstances by economic necessity.

For many Nigerians, migration is often viewed as an escape from limited opportunities at home. If economic opportunities were abundant domestically, many would choose to remain close to their families, communities, and cultural roots.

National dignity is ultimately linked to national capability.

A country that provides security, prosperity, and opportunity for its citizens commands greater respect internationally.

## Lessons From Earlier Generations

Many of Nigeria's early nationalist leaders understood a truth that contemporary discussions often overlook: political dignity is inseparable from economic power.

Figures such as Obafemi Awolowo, Nnamdi Azikiwe, and Kwame Nkrumah did not develop their political philosophies in isolation. They were products of a unique historical moment. They lived, studied, and interacted with societies that openly practiced racial segregation and colonial domination.

Nnamdi Azikiwe and Kwame Nkrumah, in particular, spent formative years in the United States during a period when racial segregation was not merely a social prejudice but an institutional reality. They witnessed a society in which access to education, housing, employment, political influence, and economic opportunity was systematically restricted along racial lines.

What they observed was not simply racism. They observed how institutions could be used to limit the economic and political advancement of entire populations.

The lesson they drew was profound: a people who do not control their economic future will ultimately remain vulnerable, regardless of formal political freedoms.

This understanding helps explain why the first generation of African nationalists placed such emphasis on education, industrialisation, economic development, and institution-building. These were not merely development policies; they were instruments of collective security and self-determination.

Awolowo's commitment to mass education illustrates this point. Education was not viewed as a social welfare programme or a political gift. It was viewed as economic infrastructure. An educated population could generate wealth, build institutions, compete globally, and resist both external domination and internal exploitation.

Their experiences under colonial administration reinforced the same conclusion. Colonial governance concentrated political authority and economic benefits in the hands of a small minority while restricting indigenous participation in meaningful decision-making. The result was a society in which power flowed upward while opportunity remained scarce.

For the nationalist generation, independence was therefore never intended to be merely the transfer of political authority from colonial officials to local elites. The objective was to construct institutions capable of producing widespread prosperity and social mobility for ordinary citizens.

In many respects, they understood something that remains relevant today: discrimination is easiest to impose on populations that lack economic leverage. The strongest response to exclusion is not simply demanding respect, but building the economic, educational, and institutional foundations that make disrespect increasingly costly.

Their generation saw race, class, and power operating together. Consequently, their response was not limited to political rhetoric. It was a project of economic emancipation.

That may be the most important lesson they left behind.


## The Responsibility of Leadership

The ultimate responsibility of any government is to expand the opportunities available to its citizens.

When leaders neglect education, undermine institutions, tolerate corruption, and fail to create economic growth, they weaken the nation's ability to protect its people both at home and abroad.

The consequences extend far beyond domestic politics.

A government that fails its citizens internally also reduces their bargaining power externally.

## Conclusion

The hostility some Nigerians encounter abroad cannot be explained solely through racism or xenophobia.

Those forces exist, but they operate within broader systems of economic power and social hierarchy.

The deeper issue is not merely how Nigerians are perceived by others. It is whether Nigeria possesses the economic strength, institutional quality, and political leadership necessary to protect the dignity of its citizens wherever they are.

Respect is not granted simply because it is deserved.

In international affairs, respect is often secured through competence, prosperity, and national strength.

The most effective response to discrimination abroad may therefore begin at home.

# What Would Make Nigerians Stay in Nigeria?

*Why solving Nigeria's emigration problem requires more than higher salaries, better roads, and improved security.*

Imagine offering a highly skilled Nigerian professional an annual salary of $100,000 to remain in Nigeria.

Would they stay?

At first glance, the answer seems obvious. For most Nigerians, that income would provide an extraordinary standard of living. They could afford a beautiful home, good cars, private education for their children, and many of the comforts associated with life in developed economies.

But the answer is not necessarily yes.

One serious medical emergency, an encounter with an unreliable security system, or a moment when their family's safety depends on institutions they cannot trust might be enough to make them reconsider.

Now imagine something different.

The same professional earns $100,000 annually, but lives in a well-planned Nigerian city. The roads are maintained, the police are professional, hospitals are reliable, schools meet international standards, and public institutions function as expected.

Would they stay now?

Certainly, the proposition becomes more attractive. But even then, a significant number might still choose to emigrate.

Why?

Because the decision to emigrate is not simply a comparison of present living conditions. It is also a comparison of possible futures.

And this is where much of Nigeria's conversation about emigration misses something important.

## The Problem Is Not Just What Nigerians Earn, but What They Can Become

A Nigerian professional considering relocation is not necessarily asking only whether they can earn more money abroad.

They are also considering the economic environment in which they can build their future.

Where can their career take them?

What kinds of companies can they work for?

What opportunities exist to develop internationally competitive skills, build businesses, accumulate wealth, and participate in global markets?

What future will their children inherit?

These questions concern something broader than income: **upward economic mobility**.

A professional earning a comfortable salary in Nigeria might still believe that relocating to London, Toronto, or New York offers access to a larger economic system, with more employers, more sophisticated industries, deeper capital markets, and greater possibilities for advancement.

Even where that belief is not always justified, it can strongly influence migration decisions.

This is why simply increasing salaries cannot be Nigeria's entire response to emigration.

We must create environments in which Nigerians can reasonably believe that remaining in the country does not require limiting their ambitions.

## What If Global Opportunities Existed in Nigerian Towns?

Consider Osun State.

Like many parts of Nigeria, Osun has towns with substantial human potential but relatively limited concentrations of large, internationally competitive businesses.

Take Gbongan, for example.

Imagine that Gbongan became home to five or six companies, each generating between $50 million and $70 million in annual revenue.

These are not businesses whose survival depends entirely on local purchasing power. They serve national and international markets, employ skilled professionals, attract suppliers, and participate in global value chains.

Together, these companies would represent between $250 million and $420 million in annual business revenue concentrated within a relatively small geographical area.

That is not the same as $250 million to $420 million entering the local economy. Revenue is not profit, local expenditure, or economic value added. But even a fraction of that activity retained locally could fundamentally change the economic prospects of a town like Gbongan.

Think about what such companies would require.

They would need engineers, accountants, technicians, managers, logistics operators, legal professionals, healthcare providers, and administrative staff.

They would receive customers, contractors, investors, foreign technical partners, and visiting executives.

They would need reliable transport, accommodation, electricity, telecommunications, financial services, and security.

Their employees would need homes, schools, hospitals, restaurants, recreational facilities, and everyday services.

And importantly, these demands would not be isolated.

They would exist in the same geographical area, reinforcing one another.

## Economic Density Creates the Conditions for Better Institutions

A single successful company can transform the fortunes of its owners and employees.

But a concentration of successful companies can begin to transform an entire locality.

When multiple businesses operate within a relatively small geographical area, their combined demand can support services that might otherwise be commercially unviable.

A well-equipped private hospital becomes more feasible when there are enough households able and willing to pay for reliable healthcare.

A quality school becomes more viable when a growing population of professionals demands better education.

Developers become more willing to invest in planned residential estates when they can anticipate sustained demand from workers, managers, and business owners.

Hotels, restaurants, transport providers, and professional service firms can justify higher standards when there is a consistent market for their services.

Even local artisans, traders, and small businesses benefit from the expanding economic activity.

A mechanic may find customers who can afford more sophisticated services. A carpenter may begin serving developers building higher-quality housing. A local food supplier may gain access to institutional buyers with larger and more predictable orders.

The effect extends beyond the companies themselves.

**The businesses create demand. The demand supports investment. The investment creates better services, institutions, and opportunities.**

This is one of the fundamental advantages of economic clustering.

It does not mean that good institutions automatically emerge wherever wealthy companies operate. Without appropriate governance, the same concentration of wealth can produce inequality, exclusion, congestion, and poorly planned development.

But it does create an economic foundation upon which stronger local institutions can be built and sustained.

## Making Nigeria Attractive to Nigerians Requires Making It Attractive to the World

There is another dimension to this argument.

Nigeria often approaches emigration as though the country's primary objective should be to convince its citizens not to leave.

But perhaps we should ask a different question:

**What would make a qualified professional from another country voluntarily choose to live and work in Gbongan, Osun State?**

Not because they were posted there temporarily, or because they had no alternative, but because the economic opportunity was genuinely attractive.

What would make an Indian engineer, a Kenyan financial professional, a British researcher, or a South African entrepreneur consider building part of their future there?

The answers are remarkably similar to what would persuade a Nigerian professional to remain.

They would need access to meaningful economic opportunities, reliable institutions, personal security, good healthcare, decent housing, and an environment in which their families could thrive.

They would also need professional networks and connections to the wider world.

If a Nigerian town can attract international talent on these terms, it has probably become considerably more attractive to its own citizens.

This changes the ambition.

Rather than merely trying to prevent Nigerians from leaving, we should build places that people, regardless of nationality, have compelling reasons to enter.

A country that can attract and retain international talent is also better positioned to retain its domestic talent.

## The Doctor Who No Longer Has to Leave

Consider a young Nigerian doctor earning $1,500 or $2,000 monthly.

In many parts of the country, that would be a meaningful income.

But the doctor's decision to stay would depend on more than their monthly pay.

Suppose they worked in a growing economic cluster where several internationally competitive businesses operated.

The surrounding population could sustain a sophisticated hospital, specialized medical services, diagnostic facilities, and a growing private healthcare market.

The doctor might see opportunities to specialize, build a medical practice, establish a diagnostic business, participate in international research, or eventually own a healthcare enterprise.

They might also have access to international professional networks without permanently relocating.

Now compare that with moving abroad.

The doctor would no longer be comparing a stagnant local career against an internationally connected one.

They would be comparing two different paths to professional and economic advancement.

The decision might still favour emigration. There are many legitimate reasons to relocate, including personal preference, family ties, and lifestyle.

But remaining in Nigeria would become a genuine competitive option rather than a sacrifice.

That distinction matters.

**The goal should not be to eliminate emigration. It should be to eliminate the feeling that emigration is a prerequisite for success.**

## Why Local Governments Would Have a Reason to Care

A concentration of productive businesses also changes the relationship between local governments and economic development.

In a locality with little formal economic activity, government revenue may depend heavily on transfers from higher levels of government and taxes or levies imposed on relatively small businesses.

But where significant businesses operate, the local economy becomes more commercially valuable.

Property development increases. Supporting businesses emerge. Employment expands. Demand for infrastructure grows.

The local government has stronger economic reasons to facilitate development, maintain public spaces, improve planning, and support commercial activity.

The state government also has an incentive to protect the conditions that make the location attractive to investors.

However, this transformation is not automatic.

It requires institutions that can translate expanding economic activity into public benefits, through effective taxation, transparent spending, land-use planning, infrastructure investment, and accountable administration.

Otherwise, a prosperous industrial enclave could exist alongside an impoverished surrounding community.

The objective must therefore be more ambitious than simply attracting large companies.

It must be to use productive economic activity as the foundation for broad-based local development.

## Nigeria Does Not Need Every Town to Become Lagos

An important implication of this argument is that economic development does not have to be concentrated in Lagos, Abuja, or a handful of major cities.

Different parts of Nigeria can develop specialized economic clusters based on their resources, geography, existing skills, and commercial advantages.

Some may specialize in agricultural processing and food manufacturing.

Others may become centres for industrial production, logistics, healthcare services, engineering, or export-oriented businesses.

What matters is not that every town hosts every industry.

It is that enough productive businesses operate near one another to create a self-reinforcing economic environment.

For a place like Gbongan, the question should not simply be how to attract residents or build more houses.

It should be what kinds of globally competitive businesses could operate there, what infrastructure they would require, and how the surrounding communities could participate in the resulting economic growth.

The businesses provide the economic engine.

Planning and institutions determine how broadly the benefits are distributed.

## From Brain Drain to Economic Attraction

Nigeria's emigration debate has largely focused on what the country is losing.

We talk about doctors leaving hospitals, engineers relocating, young graduates seeking opportunities abroad, and families moving in search of stability.

These are real problems.

But the deeper challenge is not simply that Nigerians are leaving.

It is that too many Nigerians see their country's economic environment as offering a narrower future than the alternatives available elsewhere.

Higher salaries matter. Security matters. Healthcare matters. Education matters.

None of these should be dismissed.

Yet improvements in these areas become more sustainable when they are supported by a productive economy capable of generating the wealth, demand, and public revenue needed to maintain them.

This is why Nigeria needs more than scattered investments or isolated industrial estates.

It needs geographically concentrated, internationally competitive businesses capable of creating entire local ecosystems of economic opportunity.

And it needs public institutions that can guide that growth toward better living conditions for the wider population.

Ultimately, Nigerians should not have to choose between remaining close to their families, communities, and cultural roots and participating meaningfully in the global economy.

They should be able to do both.

**The question is not simply what Nigeria must provide to stop Nigerians from leaving.**

**It is what Nigeria must become so that Nigerians, and people from elsewhere, actively want to build their futures here.**

## The Political Value of a Weak Middle Class

You have probably asked yourself this question before:

**Why am I poor?**

Perhaps not poor in the absolute sense, but poor relative to the effort you put into your life. You work. You pay taxes. You acquired an education. Yet owning a home feels increasingly out of reach. Healthcare is expensive. School fees keep rising. Savings are difficult to maintain. Every year seems to require more effort just to maintain the same standard of living.

Does this make sense?

More importantly, who benefits from it?

Most people assume poverty is simply the result of bad economic conditions, corruption, or unfortunate circumstances. While these factors matter, they often overlook a more uncomfortable possibility: what if the persistence of economic insecurity serves a political purpose?

The strength of a democracy is often measured by the strength of its middle class.

A large middle class possesses economic independence, educational attainment, professional networks, and the confidence to demand accountability from government. It is therefore one of the most important safeguards against the concentration of political power.

This reality creates a tension within poorly governed political systems.

Economic development produces independent citizens. Independent citizens are more difficult to control.

They are less dependent on political patrons for employment, less vulnerable to economic intimidation, and more willing to challenge authority when institutions fail. They can organise, litigate, vote according to their interests, and demand reforms.

A weak middle class produces the opposite outcome.

When salaries are delayed for months, households exhaust their savings and accumulate debt. When public schools deteriorate, families become dependent on expensive private alternatives. When healthcare systems collapse, citizens are forced to rely on personal networks rather than public institutions. When housing remains unaffordable, long-term economic security becomes increasingly difficult to achieve.

Across multiple Nigerian states and at various points in the country's history, prolonged salary arrears, pension backlogs, collapsing social infrastructure, and chronic underinvestment in public services have produced precisely these effects.

The consequence is not merely economic hardship. It is the gradual erosion of citizen independence.

Individuals who spend most of their time worrying about unpaid salaries, rising living costs, healthcare expenses, school fees, or personal security have less capacity to organise politically, hold leaders accountable, or advocate for institutional reform.

Whether this outcome is the result of deliberate strategy or political indifference is ultimately secondary to its effects.

The practical result is the same: a weaker middle class, a more dependent population, and a political system in which access to power becomes more important than the creation of opportunity.

This is one reason the neglect of education, healthcare, housing, and public services should not be viewed merely as administrative failures. These institutions are the foundations upon which an independent citizenry is built.

A government committed to long-term national development should seek to expand the middle class. A government comfortable with dependency may find little urgency in doing so.
